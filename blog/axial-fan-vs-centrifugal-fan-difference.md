# Axial vs Centrifugal Fans — Pressure, Airflow and Use Cases

"How does an axial fan differ from a centrifugal fan?" is one of the first
questions every buyer, OEM engineer and system designer asks when sourcing
industrial ventilation equipment. The two families look similar in product
photos, yet they behave very differently once installed. This guide compares
them across airflow, static pressure, construction, motor technology and
typical applications — and ends with a 30-second selection rule you can apply
to any project.

## Quick Answer: Airflow Path Is the Core Difference

An **axial fan** moves air parallel to the rotating shaft for **high airflow at
low static pressure**, while a **centrifugal fan** turns the airflow 90° and
builds much higher static pressure for ducted, filtered systems. Choose an
axial fan for open cooling paths; choose a centrifugal blower when air must be
pushed through resistance.

- An **axial fan** moves air **parallel to the rotating shaft** — air enters and
  exits along the same axis, like a propeller pushing air straight through.
- A **centrifugal fan** (often called a **centrifugal blower**) draws air in
  along the shaft and **expels it at 90°** through a scroll housing, using
  centrifugal force generated by a rotating impeller.

That single geometric difference drives everything else: pressure capability,
duct compatibility, physical size, noise character and cost.

## Axial Fans: High Airflow, Low Pressure

Axial fans move large air volumes efficiently as long as the resistance in
their path stays low. Because air travels straight through, the fan can be
compact — a short-cased or **flange-mounted frame** — while still delivering
thousands of m³/h of airflow.

Typical axial fan characteristics:

- **High airflow (CFM/m³/h) at low static pressure** — usually from tens up to
  a few hundred Pa.
- Compact axial length; simple panel, wall or flange installation.
- Performance drops quickly when filters, long ducts or dense heat sinks add
  resistance.
- Common uses: **electronics cooling**, control cabinets, telecom shelters,
  free-cooling intake/exhaust walls, condenser cooling and general
  **industrial ventilation**.

Modern industrial axial fans are increasingly specified with **EC motors**
(electronically commutated): an EC axial fan combines DC efficiency with
integrated speed control, runs on wide voltage ranges and reports telemetry in
advanced versions. For high-voltage applications such as megawatt charging or

## Centrifugal Fans: Pressure, Focus and Duct Resistance

A centrifugal fan accelerates air outward from the impeller tips and channels
it through a spiral scroll, converting velocity into **static pressure**. This
makes centrifugal blowers the right choice wherever air must be pushed
*through* something: filters, heat exchangers, long duct runs, or dense
equipment.

Typical centrifugal fan characteristics:

- **Medium airflow at high static pressure** — maintaining stable performance
  against hundreds of Pa of system resistance.
- **Backward-curved impellers** for high efficiency and lower noise on
  industrial grades.
- Two intake configurations: **single-inlet** (focused, compact) and
  **dual-inlet** (balanced airflow for larger duct networks) — the trade-offs
  are compared in our
  [single-inlet vs dual-inlet guide](/en/blog/dual-inlet-vs-single-inlet-centrifugal-blowers/).
- Common uses: **HVAC** air handling, cleanroom and laboratory ventilation,
  kitchen exhaust, industrial process equipment, energy-storage cabinet
  cooling and anywhere filtration is present.

## Axial Fan vs Centrifugal Fan: Side-by-Side Comparison

| Dimension | Axial Fan | Centrifugal Fan |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Airflow direction | Parallel to shaft (straight-through) | 90° turn through scroll housing |
| Airflow volume | High (large m³/h per frame size) | Medium |
| Static pressure | Low (tens to few hundred Pa) | High (stable against filters/ducts) |
| Impeller | Propeller-style blades | **Backward-curved** wheel |
| Footprint | Short axial length, flat | Larger scroll housing |
| Duct suitability | Short/open paths | Long, filtered, high-resistance paths |
| Noise character | Broadband "whoosh", rises with RPM | Lower tonal content at equal pressure duty |
| Motor options | AC, DC, **EC external rotor** | AC, DC, **EC external rotor** |
| Typical duty | Cabinet/electronics cooling, wall ventilation | HVAC AHU, cleanroom, process equipment |

## Which Motor Type: AC, DC or EC?

Both fan families share the same motor technology ladder:

- **AC external rotor motors** — proven, cost-effective, fixed speed (or
  stepped via transformer/voltage control). Still the volume choice for basic
  ventilation.
- **DC motors** — efficient and controllable, common in battery-powered and
  mobile equipment.
- **EC motors** — brushless DC electronics with integrated commutation: the
  highest efficiency (up to **IE5** with PMSM rotor designs), wide-speed
  control via PWM/0-10V/analog input, and lower motor temperature rise. EC is
  now the default recommendation for new industrial designs, and it is the

## How to Choose in 30 Seconds

Ask two questions:

1. **What is in the air's path?**
   - Nothing much (open grille, wall, short duct) → **axial fan**.
   - Filters, coils, silencers, long or complex ductwork → **centrifugal fan**.
2. **What matters more — volume or pressure?**
   - Maximum airflow per installed size → **axial fan**.
   - Stable airflow against resistance → **centrifugal fan**.

If both answers land in the middle — moderate pressure, moderate volume,
limited space — a **duct fan** (a compact cased axial unit for inline duct
installation) is often the pragmatic bridge between the two families.
