# Plastic vs Metal Volute: Which Centrifugal Fan Housing to Choose

The volute — the spiral housing around a centrifugal impeller — can be moulded from glass-filled polyamide or formed from steel. Both move air; they fail in different ways and cost differently. This comparison sets out the trade-offs so the housing is chosen by the application, not by habit.

## Quick comparison

| Criterion | Plastic volute (PA66-GF) | Metal volute (galvanised / stainless) |
|---|---|---|
| Typical weight | 30–50% lighter than steel | Heavier; needs stronger mounting |
| Corrosion | No rust; unaffected by humidity and salt mist | Galvanised resists; untreated steel corrodes |
| Continuous air temperature | Typically +60 °C to +80 °C | 200 °C+ depending on grade |
| Fire behaviour | Thermoplastic; melts and can feed a fire | Non-combustible; contains fire |
| Impact / abuse resistance | Good, but can crack on hard impact | High; dents rather than cracks |
| Inlet and outlet geometry | Molded radii and bellmouth at no extra cost | Formed; tighter radii cost more |
| Tooling cost | Higher upfront (injection mould) | Low; sheet-metal dies are cheaper |
| Cost per unit at volume | Low — amortised tooling, no finishing | Higher material and labour content |
| Noise (structure-borne) | Lower mass, less drumming | Can resonate; needs damping |
| Repairability | Replace the part | Weld or panel repair possible |

## Where plastic wins

**Range hoods and air curtains.** Grease-laden kitchen air is corrosive and the housing sits inside a consumer appliance where weight and cost matter. Moulded PA66-GF volutes resist detergents and humidity, and the molded bellmouth keeps noise down in a tight cabinet. The [SF146A dual-inlet series](/en/products/series/sf146a/) is built this way for exactly this duty.

**ERV / HRV cores.** A ventilation core is a cold, damp, condensing environment. Steel needs coating; plastic does not. Light weight also matters because the unit hangs on a wall.

**AHU and FCU modules.** When the air is filtered and below 60 °C, a molded housing removes a whole finishing step and cuts shipping weight. The [BC280 backward-curved series](/en/products/series/bc280/) is typical of this use.

**Salt-mist and coastal duty.** Marine and coastal HVAC units see chloride-laden air. Glass-filled polyamide is unaffected; galvanised steel is not.

## Where metal wins

**Hot air.** Kitchen canopy ducting, process exhaust and drying ovens run above the softening range of thermoplastics. Once continuous temperature passes roughly 80 °C, specify metal.

**Fire containment.** In grease-duct and commercial cooking applications, a thermoplastic housing can melt and add fuel to a duct fire. Metal is the code-friendly choice.

**Abrasive or particulate-laden air.** Dust and grit erode a plastic surface and can crack it on impact; steel wears predictably.

**Very large impellers.** Above roughly 400 mm, molding tools and wall thickness become impractical, and steel is the economical route.

## How the two fail

Plastic failures are usually **thermal or mechanical**: creep near the temperature limit, cracking after impact, or thread stripping at mounting points. They are predictable and avoidable by staying inside the envelope.

Metal failures are usually **corrosion or resonance**: rust at untreated seams and fixings, or a housing that drums at a particular speed. Both are solvable with the right grade and damping.

Neither failure mode is cheaper than choosing the right housing at specification time.

## Selection checklist

1. **Continuous air temperature** — under 60 °C: plastic is normally safe; 60–80 °C: check the compound datasheet; above 80 °C: metal.
2. **Fire strategy** — grease ducts and cooking canopies: metal.
3. **Corrosion environment** — coastal, marine, condensing ERV: plastic; hot and dry industrial: either.
4. **Unit weight budget** — wall-hung or ceiling-mounted appliances favour plastic.
5. **Volume** — above a few thousand units per year, molded tooling pays back quickly.
6. **Impeller size** — above ~400 mm, plan for metal.
